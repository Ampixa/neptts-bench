\begin{abstract}
Reliable evaluation of Nepali text-to-speech requires native-speaker judgments and stimuli that expose Nepali phonology, not only English-trained quality metrics. We introduce NepTTS-Bench, an open benchmark of 205 sentences, including 77 minimal pairs targeting aspiration, retroflex/dental contrasts, nasalization, gemination, and schwa deletion. We evaluate 10 TTS systems plus two human baselines with 6{,}962 MOS ratings from 180 contributing native Nepali speakers, ASR round-trip CER across six usable ASRs, SCOREQ, and a Nepali-specific MOS predictor. Human ratings place TingTing Asmita highest among TTS systems (3.55), but the top four systems have overlapping confidence intervals and individual-rater agreement is low (Krippendorff's $\alpha = 0.17$). Automated metrics diverge from native judgments: SCOREQ and ASR round-trip CER produce rankings that do not fully track native-speaker MOS, and Whisper large-v3-turbo hallucinates on Nepali. NepaliMOS, fine-tuned from IndicWav2Vec, reaches utterance-level Spearman $\rho = 0.59$ on held-out utterances and same-panel system-level $\rho = 0.90$, but leave-one-system-out validation drops to utterance-level $\rho = 0.43$ and does not recover held-out system ranking. We release the benchmark, ratings, evaluation toolkit, and checkpoint.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Text-to-speech (TTS) technology has advanced dramatically, with neural models achieving near-human naturalness for high-resource languages. However, for low-resource languages such as Nepali, with an L1 plus L2 speaker population on the order of tens of millions across Nepal, India, Bhutan, and the global diaspora, systematic TTS evaluation remains absent. No standardized benchmark exists for comparing Nepali TTS systems, making it impossible for researchers and practitioners to assess progress or select appropriate systems.

Several systems now claim Nepali support: Microsoft Edge TTS, Google Gemini Flash, ElevenLabs v3, the Nepali-specific TingTing.io, and open-source Piper TTS. However, their relative quality has not been systematically compared by native speakers.

We present NepTTS-Bench with the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A curated set of 205 sentences with 77 minimal pairs probing the major Nepali phonological contrasts (aspiration, retroflexion, nasalization, gemination, schwa deletion), supplemented with prosodic, lexical, and robustness probes (per-category counts in Appendix~\ref{appx:categories}).
\item Evaluation of 12 systems (10 TTS + 2 human baselines) using human MOS ratings from 180 contributing native speakers, SCOREQ, NepaliMOS, and ASR round-trip CER across six usable ASR systems, with cluster-bootstrap confidence intervals and inter-rater reliability (Krippendorff's $\alpha = 0.17$) reported.
\item NepaliMOS, the first Nepali MOS predictor: IndicWav2Vec fine-tuned on 1{,}697 matched TTS audio clips with mean-MOS labels aggregated from 5{,}865 individual ratings. NepaliMOS reaches same-panel system-level Spearman $\rho = 0.90$ against human MOS on the evaluated nine-system panel (vs SCOREQ's $0.40$), outperforming SCOREQ by rank-based Steiger's Z ($p = 0.046$); LOSO validation gives moderate utterance-level transfer ($\rho = 0.43$) but weak held-out system-level ranking.
\item Evidence that two widely-used English-trained speech evaluation models fail on Nepali in distinct ways: SCOREQ for MOS prediction misranks the top systems relative to human raters, and Whisper large-v3-turbo for ASR hallucinates on $2\%$ to $10\%$ of files per system, with average CER reaching $7.92$ on gTTS. This motivates language-specific evaluation.
\item A complete open-source toolkit: benchmark sentences, evaluation CLI (\texttt{neptts-eval}), web-based rating application, and all evaluation data.\footnote{GitHub: \url{https://github.com/Ampixa/neptts-bench}, HuggingFace: \url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/ampixa/neptts-bench}}
\end{enumerate}


\section{Related Work}

\subsection{TTS Evaluation}
Mean Opinion Score (MOS) remains the gold standard for TTS evaluation \citep{streijl2016mos}. Automated MOS predictors include UTMOS \citep{saeki2022utmos}, which won the VoiceMOS Challenge 2022 \citep{huang2022voicemos}, SCOREQ \citep{ragano2024scoreq} from NeurIPS 2024, and DNSMOS \citep{reddy2022dnsmos}. However, these models are trained on English data and their cross-lingual reliability is not established for low-resource Indic languages.


\subsection{ASR Round-Trip Evaluation}
ASR round-trip evaluation measures intelligibility by transcribing synthesized speech and computing error rates against the original text. The TTSDS2 benchmark \citep{minixhofer2025ttsds} demonstrated that metric selection significantly affects system rankings. Our work extends this finding to a low-resource Indic setting, where the choice of ASR drives Spearman $\rho$ between automated rankings and human MOS from $0.17$ (Whisper-small) to $0.60$ (Chirp~2).

\subsection{Low-Resource Language TTS}
Meta's MMS-TTS \citep{pratap2023mms} covers 1,100+ languages, although the Hugging Face TTS release did not expose a Nepali \texttt{npi} checkpoint in our snapshot. AI4Bharat's Indic Parler-TTS supports Nepali with emotion-specific prompts. The FLEURS multilingual speech benchmark \citep{conneau2022fleurs} provides parallel utterances across 102 languages including Nepali but is oriented toward ASR and speech translation rather than TTS evaluation. No systematic TTS benchmark exists for evaluating Nepali TTS systems specifically.

\subsection{Nepali Speech Resources}
Nepali speech resources include the OpenSLR-54 corpus and fine-tuned Wav2Vec2/XLS-R ASR models \citep{gagan2023xlsr}. NepConformer \citep{poudel2026nepconformer} is a recent Conformer-based Nepali ASR system, reporting 6.01\% CER and 23.96\% WER from a NeMo Conformer trained on SLR54 with a 128-token SentencePiece tokenizer and CTC decoding. We therefore include the shared NepConformer CTC-BPE checkpoint as the Nepali-trained ASR baseline in the round-trip panel. The Chirp2 STT model from Google provides strong Nepali transcription. For TTS, no prior work has systematically evaluated available systems. To our knowledge, no publicly released benchmark combines (i) phonologically-informed Nepali stimuli, (ii) native-speaker MOS ratings at scale, (iii) multi-ASR round-trip evaluation, and (iv) an open evaluation toolkit. NepTTS-Bench is the first to do so.

\section{NepTTS-Bench Design}

\subsection{Sentence Set}

Our benchmark comprises 205 sentences informed by Nepali phonology \citep{khatiwada2009, pokharel1989, bandhu1971}. The minimal-pair core (147 sentences in 77 pairs) probes a four-way laryngeal contrast (voiceless unaspirated, voiceless aspirated, voiced unaspirated, voiced aspirated) across five stop and affricate series at the bilabial, dental, retroflex, palatal, and velar places of articulation, a system absent in English \citep{khatiwada2009, pokharel1989}. For example, \textit{k\=am} (work) vs.\ \textit{kh\=am} (envelope) tests the velar aspiration contrast. The remaining 58 sentences probe question intonation, emotion, contrastive stress, homograph disambiguation, code-mixed and tongue-twister robustness, and common phrases. Per-category counts are in Appendix~\ref{appx:categories}.

Several caveats apply: the voiced aspirated (breathy) series is partially merged in casual speech, the retroflex--dental opposition tends to neutralize in coda position, and some contrasts (oral vs.\ nasal vowels, tatsama-driven gemination) carry lower functional load in Standard Nepali than in Hindi. We did not include breathy voice on sonorants (aspirated /m/, /n/, /l/), which a more comprehensive phonological audit would add. NepTTS-Bench is therefore best understood as an engineering benchmark with phonologically-motivated stimuli rather than a phonological study; a fully linguistic validation would require a trained phonetician's review and IPA-level transcription of all stimuli. Each pair was audited for grammatical correctness and minimal-pair validity (12 replaced, 6 corrected).

Additionally, 160 natural speech utterances from a Chirp2-transcribed Nepali YouTube corpus serve as human ceiling references.

\subsection{TTS Systems Evaluated}

We evaluate 10 TTS systems: ElevenLabs v3, Google Gemini Flash (in two prompting modes, see below), Microsoft Edge TTS (voices Hemkala and Sagar), three TingTing.io voices (Asmita, Sambriddhi, Subina), open-source Piper, and gTTS. As baselines we include human read speech (recorded by Nepali speakers) and natural spontaneous speech (Chirp~2-transcribed YouTube utterances). Gemini Flash auto-detects language from Devanagari text and tends to produce Hindi-accented pronunciation by default; we therefore evaluate two prompting modes as separate ``systems'', with \emph{Gemini (Hindi)} passing raw Devanagari with no language hint and \emph{Gemini Flash} prepending a Nepali-naming prompt. Both use \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash-preview-tts} with voice ``Kore''. The TTS-9 automated-metric set contains 1{,}729 utterances totaling 53.4 minutes of audio, with median utterance length ranging from 1.18 to 2.23 seconds by system. The full TTS configuration table (voice IDs, model versions, prompts, output formats) appears in Appendix~\ref{appx:configs}; results below are conditional on these specific configurations.


\subsection{Evaluation Methodology}

\paragraph{Human MOS.} Native Nepali speakers rate blinded audio samples on a 1--5 scale via a web application. Raters choose their available time (5--20 minutes) and receive a balanced, randomized set of stimuli across all systems. Same-sentence stimuli are spaced at least 3 positions apart to reduce fatigue. The system identity is hidden.

\paragraph{ASR Round-Trip.} We transcribe all TTS outputs using 7 ASR systems: Google Chirp~2, Meta \texttt{mms-1b-all}, XLS-R Nepali, NepConformer CTC-BPE \citep{poudel2026nepconformer}, Whisper-small, Whisper large-v2 via faster-whisper, and Whisper large-v3-turbo via MLX. We compute Character Error Rate (CER) on NFC-normalized text after stripping Devanagari and Latin punctuation. Whisper large-v3-turbo is reported as a negative finding only; per-system tables use the six usable ASRs. The Whisper and NepConformer scripts plus per-system results are released in \texttt{benchmark/results/}. \textbf{Automated MOS.} SCOREQ \citep{ragano2024scoreq} via ONNX. \textbf{Pair Discrimination.} For each minimal pair the rater hears both sentences from one TTS system and judges whether the contrast words sound different.

\paragraph{Spearman, CIs, and reliability.} We report Spearman's $\rho$ throughout (rank-based; $-1$ disagreement, $0$ no relationship, $+1$ agreement; convention: $|\rho|<0.3$ weak, $0.3$--$0.6$ moderate, $0.6$--$0.8$ strong, $>0.8$ very strong). Confidence intervals in Table~\ref{tab:human_mos} are 95\% cluster-bootstrap over raters (2{,}000 resamples), which gives intervals roughly twice as wide as naive Wald and which show that the top four TTS systems all have overlapping CIs. We compute Krippendorff's $\alpha$ on the rater-by-sample matrix and obtain $\alpha = 0.17$ (interval), well below the conventional $0.667$ threshold and below the $0.4$--$0.6$ typical of lab MOS studies. The rating task is therefore noisy at the individual-rating level although per-system means still differentiate among system tiers. We attribute the low $\alpha$ to the unscreened web-app rater pool, the absence of headphone or environment requirements, and the inherent difficulty of absolute MOS judgments.

\subsection{Whisper Large-v3-Turbo Hallucinates on Nepali}
\label{sec:whisper-large}

We additionally evaluated Whisper large-v3-turbo (via MLX) as a negative-finding ASR. Its outputs are dominated by sporadic but extreme hallucinations: across the nine TTS systems, between $2\%$ and $10\%$ of files have $\text{CER} > 1.0$ (the hypothesis is longer than the reference), with maximum CER reaching $186$ on Edge TTS Hemkala and $1{,}115$ on gTTS. Average CER is therefore highly skewed: $7.92$ on gTTS and $2.29$ on Edge TTS Hemkala, against $0.22$ to $0.64$ on the remaining systems. Median CER, by contrast, falls in $0.15$ to $0.44$ on every system, comparable to or worse than Whisper-small. Manual inspection confirms repeated tokens and inserted phrases unrelated to the input audio in the high-CER files.

This is an operationally important negative finding. Whisper large-v3-turbo is widely used as a default ASR in research pipelines, and its hallucination behavior on Nepali is more severe than ordinary ASR-choice sensitivity such as Whisper-small's weak agreement with human MOS ($\rho = 0.17$ in Table~\ref{tab:metric_vs_human}). Per-system averages computed naively over hallucinated runs are misleading. For low-resource Nepali TTS evaluation, Whisper large-v3-turbo should be used only with hallucination filtering. We exclude it from per-system reporting and recommend that future low-resource speech evaluations characterize ASR hallucination rate before relying on round-trip CER.

The agreement-with-humans story has two parts. \subsection{NepaliMOS Predictor}

The human study contains 6{,}962 individual MOS ratings/reviews. For NepaliMOS training, we use the matched TTS-audio subset: 5{,}865 rating records (5{,}804 from native-speaker registrations), aggregated into 1{,}697 unique audio-level mean-MOS labels matched to local files. We fine-tune IndicWav2Vec base \citep{javed2022indicwav2vec}; the top four transformer layers are unfrozen and a 2-layer MLP regression head ($768 \rightarrow 256 \rightarrow 1$) predicts MOS. Training uses MSE loss with Adam at differential learning rates (backbone $10^{-5}$, head $10^{-4}$), batch size 2, and a stratified 90/10 utterance-level train/validation split by MOS bucket. On held-out validation utterances ($n = 167$) we obtain Spearman $\rho = 0.59$ and Pearson $r = 0.57$. At the system level, averaging predictions over each system's audio and correlating the nine means with human MOS gives same-panel $\rho = 0.90$ ($p = 0.001$), better than SCOREQ's $\rho = 0.40$ by rank-based Steiger's Z ($Z=2.00$, $p = 0.046$). We then run LOSO validation over the 10 matched TTS systems, retraining from IndicWav2Vec for each held-out system with the same top-four-layer unfreezing and internal best-validation-loss selection. Across 1{,}697 held-out clips, LOSO gives utterance-level $\rho = 0.43$ and Pearson $r = 0.43$ (MAE $0.69$, RMSE $0.86$), but held-out system means are not ranked reliably ($\rho = 0.02$ over 10 label means; $\rho = -0.33$ against native-human MOS means on TTS-9). As an unseen-system smoke check, we generated all 205 stimuli with the public VITS checkpoint \texttt{atul10/nepali\_male\_v1} \citep{atul102024nepalivits}; NepaliMOS predicts a mean MOS of $2.57$ ($\sigma=0.35$), and NepConformer CTC-BPE gives CER $0.424$, but this system is excluded from correlation tables because no human MOS exists for that checkpoint. Performance scales with data volume (see Appendix~\ref{appx:scaling}).

The trend in Table~\ref{tab:scaling} suggests that more ratings continue to improve $\rho$ at this data scale. We do not extrapolate a specific target since five points are insufficient to fit a calibrated curve, and we did not vary architecture in this experiment so we cannot conclude that data is the dominant bottleneck.

\section{Discussion}

SCOREQ is useful as an external no-reference quality score, but its ranking should not be read as a native-speaker MOS substitute for Nepali. The largest SCOREQ--human rank gaps suggest that signal-level polish, ASR-friendly clarity, and native-listener preference are related but separable dimensions. Among TTS systems, the top four (TingTing Asmita, Piper, TingTing Subina, ElevenLabs v3) cluster within overlapping CIs and cannot be statistically separated. Two practical takeaways follow: a Nepal-based provider (TingTing) is competitive with global cloud services, and a fully open-source local model (Piper) is competitive with cloud systems for privacy-, cost-, or connectivity-constrained applications. For ASR-based evaluation, Chirp~2 ($\rho_\text{Human} = 0.60$) and MMS ($0.58$) are the practical choices; NepConformer CTC-BPE is useful as a Nepali-trained CTC baseline but does not improve human agreement ($\rho=0.30$). Steiger's $Z$ shows we cannot statistically separate Chirp~2 from Whisper-small ($\rho = 0.17$) at $n = 9$, but the practical gap is real, and Whisper large-v3-turbo's hallucination behavior (Section~\ref{sec:whisper-large}) makes it unusable without filtering.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Krippendorff's $\alpha = 0.17$ is itself the dominant limitation: web-app crowd-MOS without headphone or environment screening yields noisy individual ratings, and forced-choice comparative MOS (CMOS) under ITU-T P.808 controls would likely improve agreement substantially. The $\rho = 0.59$ NepaliMOS result is utterance-level on an utterance-disjoint but not system-disjoint split; the system-level $\rho = 0.90$ is same-panel agreement, not out-of-panel validation. LOSO gives a stricter picture: moderate utterance-level transfer ($\rho = 0.43$) but weak held-out system-level ranking, so NepaliMOS should be treated as a supplementary signal for unseen systems unless calibrated with human ratings. We did not run UTMOS or DNSMOS, so the automated-MOS unreliability finding is established only for SCOREQ in our experiments. NepConformer CTC-BPE is evaluated from the ILPRL/Jenny Poudel shared checkpoint/assets bundle and included in the ASR tables; the remaining reproducibility caveat is artifact provenance, because the bundle is not yet a public, versioned model card with immutable hashes and training manifests tied to the published chapter \citep{poudel2026nepconformer}. Separately, additional collaborator TTS systems are future additions once reproducible audio or checkpoints are available. The pair-discrimination subtask has only 54 ratings and we draw no statistical conclusions from it. The rater pool includes 207 self-declared native-speaker registrations, of whom 180 contributed the analyzed MOS ratings, without geographic or dialect metadata; the benchmark therefore reflects Standard Nepali only. TTS configurations (Appendix~\ref{appx:configs}) reflect the specific voice IDs, prompts, and parameters we used; alternative configurations may yield different results for the same provider. Detailed threats-to-validity discussion is in Appendix~\ref{appx:limitations}.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented NepTTS-Bench, the first publicly available comprehensive benchmark for Nepali TTS, with 205 sentences (77 minimal pairs), 12 systems evaluated through 6{,}962 ratings from 180 contributing native speakers (Krippendorff's $\alpha = 0.17$), SCOREQ, ASR round-trip CER across six usable ASRs, and NepaliMOS, a Nepali-specific MOS predictor that reaches same-panel system-level Spearman $\rho = 0.90$ ($p = 0.001$) against human MOS, better than SCOREQ's $\rho = 0.40$ by rank-based Steiger's Z ($p = 0.046$), but does not yet give reliable held-out system ranking under LOSO validation. SCOREQ misranks systems relative to human raters. ASR round-trip scoring is also model-dependent: Chirp~2 and MMS align best with human MOS among ASRs, NepConformer is a useful Nepali-trained CTC baseline without improving human agreement, and Whisper large-v3-turbo requires hallucination filtering. The low $\alpha = 0.17$ limits the precision with which the top four TTS systems can be ordered and motivates more controlled listening environments and forced-choice rating paradigms in low-resource TTS development.

\section{Threats to Validity (Detailed)}
\label{appx:limitations}

\paragraph{Rater pool and demographics.} Our rater pool includes 207 self-declared native-speaker registrations, of whom 180 contributed the 6{,}962 native-rater MOS scores analyzed here. This pool may not fully represent Nepal's linguistic diversity in geography, dialect, or L2 status. We treat ``native Nepali speaker'' as self-declared and do not collect demographic metadata in this version. The benchmark covers Standard Nepali stimuli only.

\paragraph{Listening environment.} Raters used the web rating application from their own devices and we do not enforce headphones, ambient noise control, or volume calibration. Standard ITU-T P.808 crowd-MOS protocols would screen for these; this is a meaningful threat to the absolute MOS values, although the relative ranking should be more robust.

\paragraph{Rater fatigue and order effects.} We space same-sentence stimuli at least three positions apart and randomize the within-rater stimulus order, but we do not analyze MOS drift across position-in-session. A non-trivial fraction of raters complete only the shorter session option (5 to 10 minutes) and longer sessions may exhibit rating compression toward the mean.

\paragraph{Same-system bias.} TTS systems are blinded to raters by stripping system identifiers, but voice characteristics (pitch, accent, prosody) are themselves identifying. Raters who learn to recognize a voice may anchor subsequent ratings of that voice. We do not currently audit this.

\paragraph{Statistical inference.} Confidence intervals in Table~\ref{tab:human_mos} are 95\% cluster-bootstrap intervals over raters with 2{,}000 resamples; Krippendorff's $\alpha$ uses interval distance on the rater-by-sample matrix.

\paragraph{Pair discrimination.} The pair-discrimination subtask has only 54 total ratings (under one rating per minimal pair), so we draw no statistical conclusions from it. We retain it in the rating application for ongoing data collection.

\paragraph{NepConformer artifact provenance and pending TTS systems.} NepConformer CTC-BPE is evaluated from the ILPRL/Jenny Poudel shared checkpoint/assets and included in the ASR tables. The remaining caveat is artifact provenance: the bundle is reproducible locally, but it is not yet a public, versioned model card with immutable hashes and training manifests tied to the published chapter \citep{poudel2026nepconformer}. Future releases should publish exact checkpoint hashes and manifest versions. Separately, we do not include other collaborator TTS systems whose audio or checkpoints were not yet available.
