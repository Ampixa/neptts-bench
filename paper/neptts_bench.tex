\begin{abstract}
Reliable evaluation of Nepali text-to-speech requires native-speaker judgments and stimuli that expose Nepali phonology, not only English-trained quality metrics. We introduce NepTTS-Bench, an open benchmark of 205 sentences, including 77 minimal pairs targeting aspiration, retroflex/dental contrasts, nasalization, gemination, and schwa deletion. We evaluate 10 TTS systems plus two human baselines with 6{,}962 MOS ratings from 180 contributing native Nepali speakers, ASR round-trip CER across six usable ASRs, SCOREQ, and a Nepali-specific MOS predictor. Human ratings place TingTing Asmita highest among TTS systems (3.55), but the top four systems have overlapping confidence intervals and individual-rater agreement is low (Krippendorff's $\alpha = 0.17$). Automated metrics diverge from native judgments: SCOREQ and ASR round-trip CER produce rankings that do not fully track native-speaker MOS, and Whisper large-v3-turbo hallucinates on Nepali. NepaliMOS, fine-tuned from IndicWav2Vec, reaches utterance-level Spearman $\rho = 0.59$ on held-out utterances and same-panel system-level $\rho = 0.90$, but leave-one-system-out validation drops to utterance-level $\rho = 0.43$ and does not recover held-out system ranking. We release the benchmark, ratings, evaluation toolkit, and checkpoint.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Text-to-speech (TTS) technology has advanced dramatically, with neural models achieving near-human naturalness for high-resource languages. However, for low-resource languages such as Nepali, with an L1 plus L2 speaker population on the order of tens of millions across Nepal, India, Bhutan, and the global diaspora, systematic TTS evaluation remains absent. No standardized benchmark exists for comparing Nepali TTS systems, making it impossible for researchers and practitioners to assess progress or select appropriate systems.

Several systems now claim Nepali support: Microsoft Edge TTS, Google Gemini Flash, ElevenLabs v3, the Nepali-specific TingTing.io, and open-source Piper TTS. However, their relative quality has not been systematically compared by native speakers.

We present NepTTS-Bench with the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A curated set of 205 sentences with 77 minimal pairs probing the major Nepali phonological contrasts (aspiration, retroflexion, nasalization, gemination, schwa deletion), supplemented with prosodic, lexical, and robustness probes (see Table~\ref{tab:categories}).
\item Evaluation of 12 systems (10 TTS + 2 human baselines) using human MOS ratings from 180 contributing native speakers, SCOREQ, NepaliMOS, and ASR round-trip CER across six usable ASR systems, with cluster-bootstrap confidence intervals and inter-rater reliability (Krippendorff's $\alpha = 0.17$) reported.
\item NepaliMOS, the first Nepali MOS predictor: IndicWav2Vec fine-tuned on 1{,}697 matched TTS audio clips with mean-MOS labels aggregated from 5{,}865 individual ratings (5{,}804 from native-speaker registrations). NepaliMOS reaches system-level Spearman $\rho = 0.90$ against human MOS on the same evaluated nine-system panel (vs SCOREQ's $0.40$), outperforming SCOREQ by rank-based Steiger's Z ($Z=2.00$, $p = 0.046$); leave-one-system-out validation shows moderate utterance-level transfer ($\rho = 0.43$) but weak held-out system-level ranking.
\item A complete open-source toolkit: benchmark sentences, evaluation CLI (\texttt{neptts-eval}), web-based rating application, and all evaluation data.\footnote{GitHub: \url{https://github.com/Ampixa/neptts-bench}, HuggingFace: \url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/ampixa/neptts-bench}}
\end{enumerate}


\section{Related Work}

\subsection{TTS Evaluation}
Mean Opinion Score (MOS) remains the gold standard for TTS evaluation \citep{streijl2016mos}. Automated MOS predictors include UTMOS \citep{saeki2022utmos}, which won the VoiceMOS Challenge 2022 \citep{huang2022voicemos}, SCOREQ \citep{ragano2024scoreq} from NeurIPS 2024, and DNSMOS \citep{reddy2022dnsmos}. However, these models are trained on English data and their cross-lingual reliability is not established for low-resource Indic languages.


\subsection{ASR Round-Trip Evaluation}
ASR round-trip evaluation measures intelligibility by transcribing synthesized speech and computing error rates against the original text. The TTSDS2 benchmark \citep{minixhofer2025ttsds} demonstrated that metric selection significantly affects system rankings. Our work extends this finding to a low-resource Indic setting, where the choice of ASR drives Spearman $\rho$ between automated rankings and human MOS from $0.17$ (Whisper-small) to $0.60$ (Chirp~2).

\subsection{Low-Resource Language TTS}
Meta's MMS-TTS \citep{pratap2023mms} covers 1,100+ languages, although the Hugging Face TTS release did not expose a Nepali \texttt{npi} checkpoint in our snapshot. AI4Bharat's Indic Parler-TTS supports Nepali with emotion-specific prompts. The FLEURS multilingual speech benchmark \citep{conneau2022fleurs} provides parallel utterances across 102 languages including Nepali but is oriented toward ASR and speech translation rather than TTS evaluation. No systematic TTS benchmark exists for evaluating Nepali TTS systems specifically.

\subsection{Nepali Speech Resources}
Nepali speech resources include the OpenSLR-54 corpus and fine-tuned Wav2Vec2/XLS-R ASR models \citep{gagan2023xlsr}. NepConformer \citep{poudel2026nepconformer} is a recent Conformer-based Nepali ASR system, implemented in NeMo and trained on SLR54 with a 128-token SentencePiece unigram tokenizer, 18 encoder layers, CTC decoding, and reported CER/WER of $6.01\%$/$23.96\%$. We therefore include the shared NepConformer CTC-BPE checkpoint as the Nepali-trained ASR baseline in the round-trip panel. The Chirp2 STT model from Google provides strong Nepali transcription. For TTS, no prior work has systematically evaluated available systems. To our knowledge, no publicly released benchmark combines (i) phonologically-informed Nepali stimuli, (ii) native-speaker MOS ratings at scale, (iii) multi-ASR round-trip evaluation, and (iv) an open evaluation toolkit. NepTTS-Bench is the first to do so.

\paragraph{Minimal Pairs.} Nepali has a four-way laryngeal contrast (voiceless unaspirated, voiceless aspirated, voiced unaspirated, voiced aspirated) across five stop and affricate series at the bilabial, dental, retroflex, palatal, and velar places of articulation, a system absent in English \citep{khatiwada2009, pokharel1989}. For example, \textit{k\=am} (work) vs.\ \textit{kh\=am} (envelope) tests the velar aspiration contrast. Several caveats apply. The voiced aspirated (breathy) series is partially merged in casual speech, the retroflex--dental opposition tends to neutralize in coda position, and some contrasts (oral vs.\ nasal vowels, tatsama-driven gemination) carry lower functional load in Standard Nepali than they do in Hindi. Our stimulus set therefore probes the full phonological grid as it is written, and native raters may legitimately treat near-merged contrasts more leniently. We did not include breathy voice on sonorants (aspirated /m/, /n/, /l/), which a more comprehensive phonological audit would add. We designed 77 minimal pairs in which the two sentences differ by exactly one phonological feature. Each pair was audited for grammatical correctness and minimal pair validity. 12 pairs were replaced and 6 corrected during the audit process.

NepTTS-Bench is best understood as an engineering benchmark with phonologically-motivated stimuli rather than as a phonological study of Nepali in its own right. The minimal-pair design lets a TTS evaluator probe specific contrasts that English-trained quality predictors are unlikely to model, but a fully linguistic validation would require a trained phonetician's review of every pair and IPA-level transcription of all stimuli, which we leave to future work.

\paragraph{Additional Categories.} We include sentences testing question intonation (rising pitch patterns), emotional expression, contrastive stress, homograph disambiguation, robustness (tongue twisters, code-mixed text), and common phrases.

Additionally, 160 natural speech utterances from a Chirp2-transcribed Nepali YouTube corpus serve as human ceiling references.

\subsection{Evaluation Methodology}

\paragraph{Human MOS.} Native Nepali speakers rate blinded audio samples on a 1--5 scale via a web application. Raters choose their available time (5--20 minutes) and receive a balanced, randomized set of stimuli across all systems. Same-sentence stimuli are spaced at least 3 positions apart to reduce fatigue. The system identity is hidden.

\paragraph{ASR Round-Trip.} We transcribe all TTS outputs with 7 ASR systems and compute Character Error Rate (CER) against normalized Devanagari reference text:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Google Chirp~2}: Speech-to-Text v2 API, model \texttt{chirp\_2}, language code \texttt{ne-NP}.
\item \textbf{Meta MMS-1b-all}: \texttt{facebook/mms-1b-all} via Hugging Face transformers with the Nepali language adapter.
\item \textbf{Wav2Vec2 XLS-R Nepali}: \texttt{gagan3012/wav2vec2-xlsr-nepali} with greedy CTC decoding.
\item \textbf{NepConformer CTC-BPE} \citep{poudel2026nepconformer}: NeMo \texttt{EncDecCTCModelBPE} checkpoint with a 128-token SentencePiece tokenizer, 16~kHz input, and checkpoint validation WER 0.2177.
\item \textbf{Whisper-small}: OpenAI \texttt{whisper} model \texttt{small}, \texttt{language="ne"}, \texttt{task="transcribe"}, fp32 inference on GPU.
\item \textbf{Whisper large-v2}: via \texttt{faster-whisper} (CTranslate2 backend), \texttt{int8\_float16}, beam size 5, no previous-text conditioning, single GPU. Chosen over large-v3-turbo because of the latter's hallucination on Nepali (Section~\ref{sec:whisper-large}).
\item \textbf{Whisper large-v3-turbo}: via MLX (Apple Silicon port) with the same Nepali language hint. Reported as a negative finding only.
\end{itemize}
Reference and hypothesis are NFC-normalized and stripped of common punctuation (Devanagari danda U+0964 and Latin punctuation) before edit-distance scoring. All evaluation scripts, including the NepConformer runner, are released with per-system raw transcriptions and CER values as JSON in \texttt{benchmark/results/}.

\paragraph{Automated MOS.} We use SCOREQ \citep{ragano2024scoreq}, a NeurIPS 2024 model for no-reference speech quality assessment in the synthetic speech domain, using ONNX inference on CPU.

\paragraph{Pair Discrimination.} For minimal pairs, raters hear both sentences from a pair for a given TTS system and judge whether the contrast words sound different (Yes/No).

\paragraph{Spearman Rank Correlation.} We report Spearman's rank correlation coefficient ($\rho$) throughout to quantify agreement between rankings: $\rho = +1$ indicates perfect monotonic agreement, $0$ no relationship, and $-1$ perfect disagreement. Unlike Pearson correlation, $\rho$ operates on ranks rather than raw values. This makes it invariant to the absolute scale of the underlying scores, which is appropriate when comparing metrics with different ranges (e.g., MOS in $[1,5]$ vs.\ CER as a percentage). We follow the convention that $|\rho| < 0.3$ is weak, $0.3$--$0.6$ moderate, $0.6$--$0.8$ strong, and $>0.8$ very strong.

\paragraph{Statistical Inference.} Confidence intervals in Table~\ref{tab:human_mos} are 95\% cluster-bootstrap intervals computed by resampling raters with replacement (2{,}000 resamples), which accounts for within-rater dependence given the 39 ratings per contributing rater on average. The resulting intervals are roughly twice as wide as naive Wald intervals would be, and they reveal that the top four TTS systems (TingTing Asmita, Piper, TingTing Subina, ElevenLabs v3) all have overlapping CIs. The qualitative ranking is robust at the gross level (gTTS clearly bottom, Nepali-specific and open-source systems near the top), but the ordering within the top four cannot be statistically distinguished and should not be over-interpreted.

\paragraph{Inter-Rater Reliability.} We compute Krippendorff's $\alpha$ on the rater-by-sample matrix using interval distance and obtain $\alpha = 0.17$ (ordinal $\alpha = 0.16$). This is well below the conventional $0.667$ threshold for tentative conclusions and below the $0.4$--$0.6$ range typical of well-controlled lab MOS studies. The rating task is therefore noisy: individual ratings disagree substantially across raters on the same audio, although per-system mean MOS still differentiates clearly among system tiers. We attribute the low $\alpha$ to the unscreened web-app rater pool, the absence of headphone or environment requirements, and the inherent difficulty of MOS as an absolute-scale judgment. Future revisions should report $\alpha$ alongside more controlled listening conditions or move toward forced-choice comparative ratings (CMOS).

Table~\ref{tab:human_mos} presents the main human evaluation results. Key observations:

\textbf{Human ceiling.} Natural speech scores 3.95, not 5.0, reflecting that even human recordings have quality variation (background noise, recording equipment). Read speech (3.64) scores lower than spontaneous speech (3.95), possibly due to the unnatural cadence of prompted reading.

\textbf{Nepali-specific systems compete.} TingTing Asmita (3.55) is the highest-rated TTS system, ahead of global cloud services including ElevenLabs v3 (3.47) and both Edge TTS voices (3.37--3.38). \textbf{gTTS is a weak baseline.} Google Translate TTS scores 2.60, far below all other systems and outside any other system's CI. In this configuration, it should be treated as a low-quality baseline rather than a competitive Nepali TTS system.

SCOREQ and human MOS disagree most clearly on systems that differ in signal polish, voice characteristics, and perceived naturalness. ElevenLabs v3 scores highest on SCOREQ (4.45) but humans rank it 4th among the nine TTS systems with SCOREQ scores ($\Delta\text{Rank} = -3$), or 6th of 12 overall when human baselines are included (3.47 MOS). Piper shows the opposite pattern, ranking 8th on SCOREQ but 2nd with humans ($\Delta\text{Rank} = +6$). We do not interpret these differences as absolute judgments about either provider. Rather, they show that SCOREQ's no-reference quality score captures dimensions that are not sufficient to predict native-speaker preference for these Nepali TTS configurations.

\paragraph{Gemini Flash: low CER, moderate human MOS.} A complementary divergence appears for Gemini Flash, which posts the lowest Whisper-small CER of any TTS system (0.372) yet a human MOS of $3.25$ (4th of nine). At the utterance level within Gemini Flash, the rank correlation between mean MOS and Whisper-small CER is essentially zero ($\rho = +0.02$, $n = 160$, $p = 0.78$); the same correlation is weakly negative for most other systems, as expected, and only zero/positive for Gemini and gTTS. This indicates that accurate ASR recovery of the text is not enough to predict native-listener MOS. The likely missing dimensions include prosody, cadence, speaker naturalness, and other perceptual cues that CER does not measure; we leave a phonetic diagnosis of those differences to future work.

\subsection{Whisper Variants on Nepali}
\label{sec:whisper-large}

\paragraph{Large-v3-turbo hallucinates.} We additionally evaluated Whisper large-v3-turbo (via MLX) as a negative-finding ASR. Its outputs are dominated by sporadic but extreme hallucinations: across the nine TTS systems, between $2\%$ and $10\%$ of files have $\text{CER} > 1.0$ (the hypothesis is longer than the reference), with maximum CER reaching $186$ on Edge TTS Hemkala and $1{,}115$ on gTTS. Average CER is therefore highly skewed: $7.92$ on gTTS and $2.29$ on Edge TTS Hemkala, against $0.22$ to $0.64$ on the remaining systems. Median CER, by contrast, falls in $0.15$ to $0.44$ on every system, comparable to or worse than Whisper-small. Manual inspection confirms repeated tokens and inserted phrases unrelated to the input audio in the high-CER files. This is a meaningful negative finding. Whisper large-v3-turbo is widely used as a default ASR in research pipelines, and its hallucination behavior on Nepali extends beyond the simple unreliability we observe for Whisper-small. Per-system averages computed naively over hallucinated runs are misleading. We therefore exclude it from per-system reporting and recommend that future low-resource speech evaluations characterize ASR hallucination rate before relying on round-trip CER.

\paragraph{Large-v2 is stable but not closer to humans.} As a stable Whisper-class baseline we ran Whisper large-v2 (via \texttt{faster-whisper}, \texttt{int8\_float16}, single GPU) on all 1{,}729 TTS-9 utterances. Hallucination is substantially less severe: between $1.6\%$ and $6.1\%$ of files per system have $\text{CER} > 1.0$, no system reaches the catastrophic $7.92$ average that v3-turbo posts on gTTS, and worst-case CER over the entire run stays bounded. Per-system average CER falls in $0.256$ to $0.481$, roughly $30\%$ lower than Whisper-small, consistent with the larger model being a stronger ASR. Despite this, large-v2's correlation with human MOS is $\rho = 0.22$ ($p = 0.58$, $n = 9$), only marginally better than Whisper-small's $\rho = 0.17$ and far below Chirp~2's $\rho = 0.60$ (Table~\ref{tab:metric_vs_human}). Large-v2 correlates well with the SCOREQ--Chirp~2--MMS external-metric cluster (Table~\ref{tab:correlations}), with $\rho = 0.78$ against SCOREQ. The reading is that more capable Whisper models give more accurate transcripts and align better with some automated metrics, but \emph{neither} Whisper variant is a reliable proxy for native-speaker MOS judgments on Nepali. For ASR-based round-trip evaluation, Chirp~2 (a Nepali-aware ASR) remains the strongest evaluated ASR choice, and a Nepali-specific MOS predictor (Section~\ref{sec:nepalimos}) is the only metric we evaluate that statistically significantly tracks human MOS.

\subsection{NepaliMOS Predictor}
\label{sec:nepalimos}

\paragraph{Motivation.} SCOREQ, the only English-trained automated MOS predictor we evaluate, reaches only $\rho = 0.40$ with human MOS at the system level (Section~\ref{sec:scoreq-unreliable-anchor}) and produces a ranking that differs from native-speaker MOS. We attribute this to a representation gap. SCOREQ is trained on English speech-quality data and learns a notion of audio fidelity, signal cleanness, codec artifacts, and additive noise, which is not sufficient to determine whether Nepali listeners prefer a given synthetic voice. The same representation gap may apply to UTMOS \citep{saeki2022utmos} and DNSMOS \citep{reddy2022dnsmos}, both English-trained, although we do not test them in this work. To close the gap we train a Nepali-specific predictor on the matched TTS-audio subset of the rating data, evaluate it on held-out utterances (the standard MOS-predictor evaluation) and on the same TTS system panel used for the human-vs-automated ranking comparison, and ask whether a small model fine-tuned on language-matched supervision can recover the human ranking that English-trained predictors miss.

\paragraph{Architecture and training.} The human study contains 6{,}962 individual MOS ratings/reviews. For NepaliMOS training, we use the matched TTS-audio subset: 5{,}865 rating records, of which 5{,}804 come from native-speaker registrations, aggregated into 1{,}697 unique audio-level mean-MOS labels matched to local files. The full human evaluation table uses the larger 6{,}962 native-rater rating set over TTS systems plus human baselines. We fine-tune IndicWav2Vec base \citep{javed2022indicwav2vec}; the top 4 transformer layers of the backbone are unfrozen and a 2-layer MLP regression head ($768 \rightarrow 256 \rightarrow 1$, with ReLU and dropout $0.1$) sits on top of mean-pooled hidden states. Training uses MSE loss with Adam at differential learning rates (backbone $10^{-5}$, head $10^{-4}$), batch size 2, and a stratified 90/10 utterance-level train/validation split by MOS bucket. We select the checkpoint by best validation loss, which lands at epoch 7 of 30. Both backbone and head weights are saved together so the artifact reproduces validation performance from the saved file alone.

\paragraph{Capacity and generalization controls.} After aggregation, the model sees 1{,}697 unique audio-level labels rather than all 6{,}962 individual rating events as separate training examples, so the central question is whether the model learns a general quality signal rather than memorizing system- or utterance-specific cues. The IndicWav2Vec base backbone has $95.0$M parameters; we freeze the feature extractor and the bottom 8 transformer layers and unfreeze only the top 4 layers ($28.4$M parameters), which together with the $197$K-parameter head gives $28.5$M trainable parameters out of $95.0$M total. The backbone updates at $10^{-5}$, an order of magnitude below the head's $10^{-4}$, so the pretrained representations move slowly. The head applies dropout $0.1$ between its two linear layers. Batch size is $2$, which produces noisy gradient estimates that act as implicit regularization at this data scale. The 90/10 train/validation split is stratified by integer MOS bucket so the validation set covers the full quality range. We do not use a fixed-epoch budget. Across 30 epochs we save the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss, which selects epoch $7$ (validation MSE $0.58$ on a 1 to 5 scale, RMSE $0.76$); validation loss does not improve further in the remaining 23 epochs, so the early-stopping criterion is binding rather than the epoch budget. Two empirical signals support the conclusion that the predictor learns rather than merely memorizes. First, on the $167$ held-out validation utterances, none of which appear in training, the predictor reaches utterance-level Spearman $\rho = 0.59$ and Pearson $r = 0.57$; a memorizing model would predict near the training-set mean and yield $\rho \approx 0$ on unseen utterances. Second, earlier training snapshots in the scaling table (Table~\ref{tab:scaling}) show monotonic improvement in $\rho$ as more ratings and matched targets are added, consistent with the model extracting more signal as supervision grows rather than fitting a fixed noisy set.

\paragraph{Results.} On the held-out validation utterances ($n = 167$) we obtain utterance-level Spearman $\rho = 0.59$ and Pearson $r = 0.57$. At the system level, where we average NepaliMOS predictions over all of a system's audio files and correlate the resulting nine system-level means with human MOS, we obtain $\rho = 0.90$ ($p = 0.001$). This system-level result is evidence that NepaliMOS recovers the human ranking on the evaluated TTS panel: it more than doubles SCOREQ's $\rho = 0.40$ on the same set of nine systems and is the only automated metric whose system-ranking agreement with humans reaches statistical significance at $\alpha = 0.05$ (Table~\ref{tab:metric_vs_human}). Rank-based Steiger's Z gives $Z=2.00$ and two-sided $p=0.046$ for NepaliMOS vs SCOREQ.

We also run leave-one-system-out (LOSO) validation over the 10 matched TTS systems. For each fold, one system is held out completely, NepaliMOS is retrained from IndicWav2Vec on the remaining systems with the same top-four-layer unfreezing, batch size, and differential learning rates, and the checkpoint is selected by an internal stratified validation split with a 30-epoch cap and patience of 6. Across the 1{,}697 held-out clips, LOSO gives utterance-level Spearman $\rho = 0.43$ and Pearson $r = 0.43$ (MAE $0.69$, RMSE $0.86$). However, averaging predictions within each held-out system does not recover the system ranking: Spearman $\rho = 0.02$ over the 10 audio-label means, and $\rho = -0.33$ against native-human MOS means on the evaluated TTS-9 subset. The LOSO runs tend to regress system means toward the panel center, overpredicting lower-rated gTTS and TingTing Sambriddhi while underpredicting high-rated Piper and ElevenLabs. We therefore treat NepaliMOS as a useful language-matched utterance-level quality signal and same-panel analysis tool, not as a calibrated final MOS for a new TTS system without additional system-diverse training or a small human calibration set.

\paragraph{Unseen-system smoke check.} To exercise the predictor on a TTS checkpoint absent from the human-rated panel, we generated all 205 NepTTS-Bench stimuli with the public Apache-2.0 VITS checkpoint \texttt{atul10/nepali\_male\_v1} \citep{atul102024nepalivits}. NepaliMOS assigns this unseen system a mean predicted MOS of $2.57$ (standard deviation $0.35$ over 205 utterances). NepConformer CTC-BPE also scores it weakly among the systems we transcribed (CER $0.424$, WER $0.856$). We report this only as a deployment-path smoke check: because no native-speaker MOS has been collected for this checkpoint, it is excluded from all human-correlation tables and cannot validate out-of-panel system-level ranking by itself. A small human calibration set remains necessary before using NepaliMOS as a final score for new systems.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{SCOREQ Does Not Substitute for Native MOS}
\label{sec:scoreq-unreliable-anchor}
SCOREQ is useful as an external no-reference quality score, but its ranking should not be read as a native-speaker MOS substitute for Nepali. The largest SCOREQ--human rank gaps suggest that signal-level polish, ASR-friendly clarity, and native-listener preference are related but separable dimensions. This gap motivates language-specific MOS predictors like NepaliMOS and argues for keeping native-speaker evaluation as the anchor.

\subsection{Nepali-Specific Systems Are Competitive}
TingTing.io, a Nepal-based TTS provider, achieves the highest human MOS among TTS systems (3.55). This demonstrates that language-specific training can compensate for smaller model scale, and has practical implications for applications targeting Nepali speakers.

\subsection{Open-Source Quality}
Piper TTS (3.50) is fully open-source and runs locally without cloud dependency. Its competitive performance, statistically indistinguishable from ElevenLabs and Edge TTS within our CIs, makes it viable for applications where privacy, cost, or connectivity constraints preclude cloud services.

\subsection{ASR Model Selection}
Across the six usable ASRs reported in Table~\ref{tab:metric_vs_human}, the Spearman correlation with human MOS ranges from $\rho = 0.17$ (Whisper-small) to $\rho = 0.60$ (Chirp~2), with MMS at $0.58$, XLS-R Nepali at $0.53$, NepConformer CTC-BPE at $0.30$, and Whisper large-v2 at $0.22$. With $n = 9$ TTS systems, none of these ASR correlations reach significance at $\alpha = 0.05$ and Steiger's Z test confirms that we cannot statistically distinguish Chirp~2's agreement with humans from Whisper-small's ($Z = 1.09$, $p = 0.28$). The differences are nonetheless practically meaningful: Chirp~2 places ElevenLabs and gTTS in roughly the right human-rank slots, while neither Whisper variant does. NepConformer CTC-BPE gives lower absolute CERs than the Whisper baselines on most systems, but its system ranking does not move closer to human judgments. Combined with Whisper large-v3-turbo's hallucination behavior (Section~\ref{sec:whisper-large}), our recommendation for Nepali TTS evaluation is: Chirp~2 or MMS as the primary ASR, NepConformer CTC-BPE as a useful Nepali-trained CTC baseline, Whisper large-v2 if a Whisper-class baseline is required, and Whisper large-v3-turbo avoided unless paired with hallucination filtering.

\subsection{Limitations}

\paragraph{Rater pool and demographics.} Our rater pool includes 207 self-declared native-speaker registrations, of whom 180 contributed the 6{,}962 native-rater MOS scores analyzed here. This pool may not fully represent Nepal's linguistic diversity in geography, dialect, or L2 status. We treat ``native Nepali speaker'' as self-declared and do not collect demographic metadata in this version. The benchmark covers Standard Nepali stimuli only.

\paragraph{Listening environment.} Raters used the web rating application from their own devices and we do not enforce headphones, ambient noise control, or volume calibration. Standard ITU-T P.808 crowd-MOS protocols would screen for these; this is a meaningful threat to the absolute MOS values, although the relative ranking should be more robust.

\paragraph{Rater fatigue and order effects.} We space same-sentence stimuli at least three positions apart and randomize the within-rater stimulus order, but we do not analyze MOS drift across position-in-session. A non-trivial fraction of raters complete only the shorter session option (5 to 10 minutes) and longer sessions may exhibit rating compression toward the mean.

\paragraph{Same-system bias.} TTS systems are blinded to raters by stripping system identifiers, but voice characteristics (pitch, accent, prosody) are themselves identifying. Raters who learn to recognize a voice may anchor subsequent ratings of that voice. We do not currently audit this.

\paragraph{Statistical inference and inter-rater reliability.} Confidence intervals in Table~\ref{tab:human_mos} are 95\% cluster-bootstrap intervals over raters and the Krippendorff's $\alpha$ on the rater-by-sample matrix is $0.17$. The low $\alpha$ is itself a finding: web-app crowd-MOS without environment screening or rater training yields noisy individual ratings, even though per-system means still differentiate clearly among system tiers. Forced-choice comparative MOS (CMOS) and ITU-T P.808-style screening would likely improve $\alpha$ substantially.

\paragraph{NepaliMOS predictor.} The predictor reaches utterance-level $\rho = 0.59$ on a held-out validation split that is utterance-disjoint but not system-disjoint. The system-level $\rho = 0.90$ is same-panel agreement on the evaluated TTS-9 systems, not out-of-panel validation. The LOSO experiment is the stricter test: it gives moderate utterance-level transfer ($\rho = 0.43$ across 1{,}697 held-out clips) but weak held-out system-level ranking ($\rho = 0.02$ over 10 label means and $\rho = -0.33$ against native-human MOS means on TTS-9). NepaliMOS should therefore be used as a supplementary signal for unseen systems unless calibrated with human ratings.

\paragraph{Pair discrimination.} The pair-discrimination subtask has only 54 total ratings (under one rating per minimal pair on average), so we do not draw statistical conclusions from it. We retain it in the rating application for ongoing data collection.

\paragraph{Automated MOS scope.} We did not run UTMOS or DNSMOS, so the automated-MOS unreliability finding is established only for SCOREQ in our experiments. Whether the failure generalizes to other English-trained predictors is an open question.

\paragraph{NepConformer artifact provenance and pending TTS systems.} NepConformer CTC-BPE is evaluated from the ILPRL/Jenny Poudel shared checkpoint/assets and included in the ASR tables. The remaining caveat is artifact provenance: the bundle is reproducible locally, but it is not yet a public, versioned model card with immutable hashes and training manifests tied to the published chapter \citep{poudel2026nepconformer}.

\paragraph{Configuration sensitivity.} Several TTS configurations in Table~\ref{tab:tts_configs} reflect the specific settings we used. Alternative voice IDs, prompts, or generation parameters may yield meaningfully different results for the same provider; our ranking claims are therefore conditional on these configurations and not on the providers in general.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented NepTTS-Bench, the first publicly available comprehensive benchmark for Nepali TTS evaluation, with 205 sentences, 77 minimal pairs, 12 systems evaluated through 6{,}962 ratings from 180 contributing native speakers (Krippendorff's $\alpha = 0.17$), SCOREQ, ASR round-trip CER across six usable ASRs, and NepaliMOS, a Nepali-specific MOS predictor that reaches same-panel system-level Spearman $\rho = 0.90$ ($p = 0.001$) against human MOS, better than SCOREQ's $\rho = 0.40$ by rank-based Steiger's Z ($p = 0.046$). Three findings follow. First, SCOREQ, the only English-trained automated MOS predictor we evaluate, misranks systems relative to human raters. Second, a small Nepali-specific predictor fine-tuned from IndicWav2Vec on the matched TTS rating subset recovers the evaluated panel's human ranking but does not yet provide calibrated held-out system ranking under LOSO validation. Third, ASR round-trip scoring is highly model-dependent: Chirp~2 and MMS align best with human MOS among the ASRs, NepConformer provides a useful Nepali-trained CTC baseline without improving human agreement, and Whisper large-v3-turbo requires hallucination filtering. The low individual-rating agreement limits the precision with which the top four TTS systems can be ordered and motivates more controlled listening environments and forced-choice rating paradigms in low-resource TTS development.

All resources are publicly available: the benchmark sentence set, evaluation toolkit (installable via \texttt{pip install neptts-eval}), rating application, model checkpoint, and evaluation data.
